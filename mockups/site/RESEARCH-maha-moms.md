# What MAHA moms respond to (research brief)

Compiled 2026-11-12 from web search results. Two primary pages (Center for Food Integrity, Anchorage Daily News reprint of the Washington Post) could not be opened from this session, so their findings come from search summaries. Verify figures against the original sources before quoting them publicly.

**Scope note.** No source found isolates "MAHA moms" as a measured consumer segment with purchase data. The picture below combines general data on how moms buy, survey data on MAHA-aligned attitudes, and reporting on the movement's online culture. Much of the culture coverage is commentary or advocacy, so treat it as directional.

## Findings

### Trust comes from other moms, reviews and real people
- Word of mouth is described as the single strongest purchase driver for moms (MediaPost, Apr 2026).
- 90% of moms read at least one review before buying; 72% say they would pay more for products they believe are safer for their baby (press release, Apr 2026).
- Moms check about 3.3 sources per mother and baby purchase, and 58% say information overload creates pressure (NielsenIQ 2026).
- The Center for Food Integrity's "Authenticity Seeker" segment (14% of the population, 18% of the online conversation) responds to real people, real practices and real tradeoffs. Support pulls back when messaging feels too polished, corporate, partisan or politically charged. "Comfort Seekers" (43%) want clear, reassuring guidance.

### Ingredients they watch
- Ipsos: about three in four moms see chemicals and unsafe additives in food as a large or moderate health risk, about 20 points above the general population.
- Removing petroleum-based (synthetic) dyes is the most broadly supported MAHA food idea at 76% (farmdoc daily / Gardner surveys, 2025). Replacing seed oils with animal fats is far more divisive (31% overall, 52% among supporters).
- Food makers and retailers are moving on synthetic colors (PepsiCo, Mars, General Mills, Kraft Heinz commitments; Target's synthetic-color-free cereal requirement from May 2026).
- "Clean" and "natural" have no regulated definition, so claims built on those words carry legal risk.

### Electrolytes specifically
- Reviewers and parent advocacy sites flag third-party testing for heavy metals (concentrated salts can carry them) as a key trust signal, and at least one brand publishes per-batch lab results. A parent advocacy site reported lead and arsenic in one electrolyte powder.
- USDA Organic and NSF Certified for Sport are named as helpful certifications.

### Visual and cultural cues
- Coverage describes golden-hour light, calm minimal kitchens, homesteading, gardens, fresh air and family, and a polished, purity-focused look that performs well online.
- The audience spans "crunchy" natural-living moms of different politics; partisan or flag-waving cues risk losing the Authenticity Seekers.

## What this means for Fortified's site
1. **Warm, calm, real.** Golden-hour kitchen tones, soft serif headlines in sentence case, real photography of real routines. No shouting caps, no tech gloss, no partisan or patriotic symbols.
2. **Lead with the label in plain words.** Every ingredient named with its source, plus a clear "never in our packs" list. Keep it short to fight information overload.
3. **Proof from people.** Reviews and mom stories near the buy button, founder story, customer photos. All placeholders until real.
4. **Publish testing.** Add per-batch heavy metal results if Fortified has them. This is the strongest trust signal in the category and it is currently missing from the site.
5. **Reassure on control.** Skip, swap and cancel online, money-back guarantee, plain renewal terms.

## Claims to confirm before launch
- "No synthetic dyes": the published ingredient list contains none, but confirm with the approved label and supplier specs.
- Any testing, certification (USDA Organic, NSF) or "safe for kids" or pregnancy language: do not use until Fortified has the evidence and legal sign-off. FTC requires substantiation for health and safety claims, and FDA rules govern supplement structure and function claims; both change, so check current guidance.

**Pattern:** a bright warm cream base (not grey beige), one deep earthy anchor (forest, moss, denim, brown or near-black) for text and buttons, and one or two confident food-colored accents (tomato, lime, peach, caramel). Real, warm product and food photography carries most of the color. Nobody in this set leans on muted all-beige pages.

**What Fortified should take from it:** keep the bright cream base, use its own navy #0b1c29 as the deep anchor (it sits naturally next to Ballerina Farm's denim and Redmond's deep teal), and let the warm orange from the packs be the confident accent, with peach as the soft wash. Teal stays as a small brand mark.
